1.1Clinical Outcomes and Priorities

A clinical trial can tell more than one story about a patient’s outcome. In heart failure, survival matters, but so do hospitalizations and their recurrence. In cancer, a patient may experience relapse, progression, and ultimately death. A composite endpoint brings several outcomes together through a specified rule.

The rule is substantive: it determines which features of the patient’s experience enter the analysis and how they contribute to the treatment comparison. The first chapter uses colon cancer and HF-ACTION to motivate this choice.

Begin with the clinical question.

Which outcomes matter, how should they be combined, and what population-level treatment effect should the analysis estimate?

1.2From First Events to Hierarchies

Time to first event reduces the outcome to the earliest occurrence of a component event. It supports familiar survival methods, but subsequent events do not contribute to that endpoint, and the rule does not give a fatal event priority over an earlier nonfatal event.

Weighted total events retain event counts and assign relative weights to the components. The choice of weights expresses how different events contribute to the composite burden.

Hierarchical comparisons compare patients sequentially according to clinical priority. Under a survival-first rule, a decisive survival comparison determines the winner. If survival does not decide the comparison, the specified nonfatal-event rule is considered.

WR̂=treatment winscontrol wins \widehat{\mathrm{WR}} = \frac{\text{treatment wins}}{\text{control wins}}

The win ratio summarizes these pairwise comparisons. Win odds and net benefit provide related summaries with different treatment of ties and different effect scales. See Pocock et al. (2012) for the original win ratio formulation.

1.3Why the Time Horizon Matters

Win–loss status can change as the comparison horizon changes. A treatment patient might initially fare better on hospitalization but later lose the comparison on survival. This makes follow-up time part of the substantive interpretation.

In standard censored-data comparisons, the available follow-up determines the time at which pairs are compared. Without additional structure, the resulting summary can depend on the censoring distribution. Valid hypothesis testing and estimation of a specified treatment effect are therefore distinct tasks.

The course develops two responses: define a common restriction time and estimate the corresponding effect, or specify a model for the time-dependent win fractions. Chapters 3 and 4 examine these approaches.

A recurring distinction

Evidence that the groups differ is not the same as an interpretable estimate of how much they differ. Keep the estimand, assumptions, and estimator separate.

For the detailed argument, see Mao (2024), “Defining estimand for the win ratio.”
